Historical Summary of the Period

When and who. Ezekiel lived during the darkest stretch of Judah’s history. Babylon, under King Nebuchadnezzar II (reigned 605–562 BC), had replaced Assyria as the superpower of the Near East. When Judah rebelled, Nebuchadnezzar besieged Jerusalem, and in 597 BC he captured the city and carried off the young King Jehoiachin along with princes, soldiers, and skilled craftsmen—about ten thousand people (2 Kings 24:12–16). Ezekiel, a priest and “the son of Buzi,” was one of them (Ezekiel 1:3).

Where. The exiles were not locked in prison camps. Babylon resettled them in farming villages along the irrigation canals of southern Mesopotamia, roughly 900 miles from Jerusalem by the caravan route. Ezekiel lived at Tel-abib by the river Chebar (1:1; 3:15). The Hebrew word for “river” can also mean a large canal, and most scholars connect the Chebar with a major canal near the city of Nippur. Ezekiel had his own house where the elders of Judah came to sit with him (8:1), just as Jeremiah had counseled the exiles to build houses and plant gardens (Jeremiah 29:5–7).

Two stages of the story. Ezekiel was called in the fifth year of Jehoiachin’s captivity (about 593 BC). For his first seven years, many exiles believed Jerusalem would never fall and they would soon go home. Ezekiel’s early chapters warned them otherwise. In 586 BC the Babylonians burned the temple, and in Ezekiel 33:21 a refugee arrives with the news: “The city is smitten.” From that moment Ezekiel’s message turns from warning to hope—a new heart, a gathered people, a living river, and a new temple.

Culture and faith. As a priest, Ezekiel thought in temple language: glory, holiness, cleanness, sanctuary. Exile was a crisis of faith—had Israel’s God been defeated by Babylon’s gods? Ezekiel’s first vision answered that question: the throne of God came to him on wheels, in Babylon. The Lord was not trapped in a building, and He had not forgotten His people. Ezekiel 1:1 begins “in the thirtieth year,” which many scholars think was Ezekiel’s own age—the age when priests began temple service (Numbers 4:3). The man trained for the temple received his calling with no temple at all.

Archaeology anchors

Jehoiachin’s ration tablets. German excavators at Babylon (1899–1917), digging near the Ishtar Gate, found cuneiform tablets listing oil rations for “Ya’u-kinu, king of the land of Yahudu” and his five sons. The one with a preserved date comes from Nebuchadnezzar’s 13th year (592/591 BC)—the very years Ezekiel was dating his prophecies by “king Jehoiachin’s captivity” (1:2). Babylon still called him “king.” (Pergamon Museum, Berlin.)

The Al-Yahudu tablets. About 200 clay contracts (572–477 BC) from a Babylonian village named Al-Yahudu—“Judah-town.” They record rent, taxes, and marriages of Judean exiles with Hebrew names built on the Lord’s name, and they mention the Chebar canal. Older tablets from the Murashu family archive at Nippur (5th century BC) also name the nāru kabari, the “great canal.”

Ezekiel at a glance

The book’s dates run from about 593 BC (1:2) to about 571 BC (29:17). Chapters 1–24: warnings before Jerusalem falls. Chapters 25–32: messages to the nations. Chapters 33–48: hope and restoration, ending with a vision of a new temple and a city named “The LORD is there” (48:35). See “Ezekiel” in the Bible Dictionary.

Passage Summary

This week’s reading jumps through the book. Here is what happens in each block, in order.

1
Ezekiel 1

The glory of God by the Chebar

A storm cloud flashing with fire rolls in from the north. Inside are four living creatures with four faces (man, lion, ox, eagle) and wings, beside wheels “in the middle of a wheel” full of eyes. Above them is a crystal expanse, a sapphire throne, and on it a figure “as the appearance of a man,” surrounded by brightness like a rainbow. Ezekiel recognizes “the likeness of the glory of the LORD” and falls on his face (1:28).

2
Ezekiel 2:1–3:15

Called, filled, and fed

The Lord says, “Son of man, stand upon thy feet,” and the Spirit enters him (2:1–2). He is sent to a “rebellious house” whether they listen or not (2:5). He is handed a scroll written with lamentation and woe and told to eat it; it tastes “as honey for sweetness” (3:3). The Lord makes his forehead harder than flint so he won’t be intimidated (3:8–9). He sits stunned among the exiles at Tel-abib for seven days.

3
Ezekiel 3:16–21

Appointed a watchman

The Lord makes Ezekiel “a watchman unto the house of Israel” (3:17). If he warns people and they ignore him, he has “delivered his soul.” If he stays silent, he shares the responsibility for what happens to them.

4
Ezekiel 33:1–20

“How should we then live?”

The watchman calling is repeated. The exiles, weighed down by their sins, ask how they can possibly live (33:10). The Lord answers that He takes no pleasure in the death of the wicked: “Turn ye, turn ye from your evil ways” (33:11). A person who turns from sin and makes things right will live, and “none of his sins … shall be mentioned unto him” (33:16). But a person who trusts in past goodness while choosing sin cannot hide behind it.

5
Ezekiel 33:21–33

The news arrives

A survivor reports that Jerusalem has fallen. Ezekiel’s neighbors start coming to hear him—but they treat his words like “a very lovely song” and then don’t do them (33:32).

6
Ezekiel 34

Bad shepherds and the Good Shepherd

The Lord condemns Israel’s leaders, who fed themselves instead of the flock. They didn’t strengthen the weak, heal the sick, bind up the broken, bring back the driven away, or seek the lost (34:4). So the Lord Himself will search for His sheep, feed them in “a good pasture,” and seek the lost (34:11–16). He will set up “one shepherd … my servant David” (34:23) and send “showers of blessing” (34:26).

7
Ezekiel 36

A new heart

The Lord will gather Israel from every country, sprinkle clean water on them, and give them “a new heart” and “a new spirit,” taking away the “stony heart” and giving “an heart of flesh” (36:24–26). He will put His Spirit within them (36:27), and the ruined land will become “like the garden of Eden” (36:35).

8
Ezekiel 37:1–14

The valley of dry bones

In vision, Ezekiel walks among very dry bones. “Can these bones live?” “O Lord GOD, thou knowest.” As he prophesies, there is a noise and a shaking; bones come together, then sinews, flesh, and skin, and finally breath—“an exceeding great army.” The Lord explains: Israel says “our hope is lost,” but He will open their graves, put His Spirit in them, and bring them home (37:11–14).

9
Ezekiel 37:15–28

Two sticks become one

Ezekiel writes “For Judah” on one stick and “For Joseph, the stick of Ephraim” on another and joins them “into one stick” in his hand (37:16–17). The Lord will make His people one nation under one king, “David my servant,” with an everlasting covenant of peace and His sanctuary among them forever (37:24–28).

10
Ezekiel 47:1–12

A river from the temple

Water flows from under the threshold of the temple. Ezekiel’s guide measures it: ankle-deep, knee-deep, waist-deep, then a river too deep to cross (47:3–5). It flows into the Dead Sea and heals it, so fishermen spread nets from En-gedi to En-eglaim (47:10). Trees on its banks never lose their leaves, bear fruit every month, and their leaves are “for medicine” (47:12).

Hard question: “Isn’t Ezekiel 33:12–13 unfair?”

Students may ask why a good person’s past goodness “shall not be remembered” if he turns to sin. Ezekiel isn’t describing a scoreboard where good points cancel bad ones; he’s describing direction. The Lord cares about who we are becoming. To “trust to [your] own righteousness” means treating past obedience as a permission slip to sin now. The same principle is good news for the person who has made mistakes: when they turn around, their sins are “not mentioned.” Compare the two sons in Matthew 21:28–31 and the Pharisee and publican in Luke 18:9–14.

Why “son of man”?

The Lord calls Ezekiel “son of man” about 90 times. In Hebrew (ben adam) it simply means “human being” or “mortal”—a reminder of how small Ezekiel was next to the glory he had seen. It is different from the title “Son of Man” used for Jesus Christ (see Moses 6:57). This is one of this week’s Scripture Helps questions.

“A new heart also will I give you, and a new spirit will I put within you.”Ezekiel 36:26

Book of Mormon Parallels

Ezekiel and the Book of Mormon prophets were living at almost the same moment—Lehi left Jerusalem around 600 BC, just a few years before Ezekiel was taken to Babylon. They share the same patterns.

Ezekiel

Ezekiel eats the scroll and it is sweet as honey (Ezekiel 2:8–3:3).

Book of Mormon

Lehi is given a book to read and is filled with the Spirit (1 Nephi 1:11–12). Nephi invites us to “feast upon the words of Christ” (2 Nephi 32:3).

Teaches: God’s word has to get inside us before it can come out of us.
Ezekiel

The watchman must warn, or the people’s blood is on him (Ezekiel 3:17–21; 33:1–9).

Book of Mormon

Jacob taught diligently so he wouldn’t answer for the people’s sins (Jacob 1:19). King Benjamin spoke so he could be “rid of [their] blood” (Mosiah 2:27–28).

Teaches: Callings come with real responsibility to warn and teach—out of love.
Ezekiel

“How should we then live?” The Lord wants the wicked to turn and live (Ezekiel 33:10–16).

Book of Mormon

“As often as my people repent will I forgive” (Mosiah 26:30). “Now is the time … to prepare to meet God” (Alma 34:31–33).

Teaches: The Lord is eager to forgive; repentance is turning, not just feeling bad.
Ezekiel

False shepherds fed themselves, not the flock (Ezekiel 34:1–10).

Book of Mormon

Alma’s priests worked with their own hands to support themselves (Mosiah 18:24, 26), and the Good Shepherd calls His sheep by name (Alma 5:37–39).

Teaches: Real leaders serve the flock instead of using it.
Ezekiel

“One shepherd” will gather the scattered sheep (Ezekiel 34:23; 37:24).

Book of Mormon

The risen Christ tells the Nephites they are His “other sheep”: there will be “one fold, and one shepherd” (3 Nephi 15:21; 16:3).

Teaches: Jesus Christ is gathering every one of His flocks, everywhere.
Ezekiel

A new heart replaces the heart of stone (Ezekiel 36:26).

Book of Mormon

King Benjamin’s people felt “a mighty change in us, or in our hearts” (Mosiah 5:2). Alma asks, “Have ye experienced this mighty change?” (Alma 5:14, 26).

Teaches: Real change is a heart transplant only the Savior can do.
Ezekiel

Dry bones receive sinews, flesh, skin, and breath (Ezekiel 37:1–14).

Book of Mormon

Every limb and joint will be restored to its “proper and perfect frame” (Alma 11:43–44; 40:23; see 2 Nephi 9:12–13).

Teaches: The Resurrection is literal and for everyone.
Ezekiel

Two sticks, Judah and Joseph, joined into one (Ezekiel 37:15–20).

Book of Mormon

Joseph of Egypt was told the writings of Judah and of Joseph would “grow together” (2 Nephi 3:12); the later records would establish the truth of the first (1 Nephi 13:40–41).

Teaches: The Bible and Book of Mormon are two witnesses of Jesus Christ.
Ezekiel

A river from the temple heals the Dead Sea and feeds trees of life (Ezekiel 47:1–12).

Book of Mormon

Nephi sees that the “fountain of living waters” and the tree of life represent the love of God (1 Nephi 11:25; see 1 Nephi 8:10–13).

Teaches: God’s love flows from His presence and heals what seems dead.
Ezekiel

“They hear thy words, but they do them not” (Ezekiel 33:31–32).

Book of Mormon

King Benjamin: “if ye believe all these things see that ye do them” (Mosiah 4:10).

Teaches: Liking a lesson isn’t the same as living it.

Tie to Come, Follow Me

This week’s outline is titled “A New Spirit Will I Put within You” (November 2–8; Ezekiel 1–3; 33–34; 36–37; 47). Its introduction stresses that Ezekiel saw the Lord’s glory in Babylon, among the exiles—and that no matter how far Israel had fallen, what was dead could be revived.

The outline’s principles

  • The Lord invites me to feed His sheep (Ezekiel 3; 34). The manual asks what the titles “watchman” and “shepherd” suggest about leading; who the “sheep” are that the Lord wants you to feed; what “good pasture” and “good fold” might represent (34:14); and the difference between a sheep that is “lost” and one that is “driven away” (34:16). See John 21:15–17 and Elder Gerrit W. Gong, “Ministering” (April 2023).
  • The Lord wants to forgive (Ezekiel 33:10–19). Questions: What does it mean to “trust to [your] own righteousness” (33:12–13)? What would you say to someone who thinks these verses are unfair (compare Matthew 21:28–31; Luke 18:9–14)? What do Ezekiel 36:26–27 and Alma 7:14–16 add to your understanding of repentance?
  • The Lord is gathering His people and giving them new life (Ezekiel 37; seminary focus). Consider the dry bones (37:1–14; see 36:24–30) as gathering on both sides of the veil, and the two sticks (37:15–28) as the Bible and Book of Mormon. The manual notes many scholars see the sticks as hinged wooden writing boards. See 2 Nephi 3:11–13; 29:14; “Israel, Israel, God Is Calling” (Hymns, no. 7); video “The Book of Mormon Gathers Scattered Israel.”
  • I can find spiritual healing in the house of the Lord (Ezekiel 47:1–12). The Dead Sea is so salty fish and plants can’t live in it. What might the waters and trees symbolize (see Revelation 22:1)? See Elder Dale G. Renlund, “Family History and Temple Work: Sealing and Healing” (April 2018).

Latter-day connections

Doctrine and Covenants 138:43: In President Joseph F. Smith’s vision of the spirit world, Ezekiel is named as the prophet “shown in vision the great valley of dry bones” that would rise in the Resurrection.

Doctrine and Covenants 27:5: The Lord calls the Book of Mormon “the stick of Ephraim,” whose record keys were committed to Moroni.

Ezekiel 37:16, footnote a (Church edition of the Bible) explains “stick” as a wooden writing tablet. In 1953 archaeologist Max Mallowan found a set of sixteen hinged ivory writing boards in a well at Nimrud, Assyria, and BYU professor Keith H. Meservy connected such boards to Ezekiel’s sticks (Ensign, Sept. 1977 and Feb. 1987). Some scholars question that identification, so present it as a strong possibility, not a proof.

Doctrine and Covenants 88:81: “It becometh every man who hath been warned to warn his neighbor”—the watchman idea extended to every member.

Teaching ideas for ages 12–13

  • Two boards demo. Tape two thin boards or pieces of cardboard together with a duct-tape hinge. Label one “For Judah” and one “For Joseph.” In pairs, have students find one Bible verse and one Book of Mormon verse that teach the same truth about Christ (try John 10:11 with Alma 5:38, or Matthew 5:3–12 with 3 Nephi 12:3–12).
  • How deep is the river? Read Ezekiel 47:3–5 slowly while everyone touches ankle, knee, waist, then “swims.” Ask: How do temple blessings grow the more we return? What “dead” things in a person’s life can that water heal?
  • Dry bones, live. Assign readers for 37:1–10 and let the class add sound effects—rattling, shaking, wind. Then read 37:11: Who might feel like “our hope is lost”? What does the Lord promise them?
  • Lost vs. driven away. List on the board reasons someone might stop coming to church or youth activities. Sort them: “lost” (wandered off) or “driven away” (someone pushed them). What would a good shepherd do differently for each? Invite each student to choose one person to reach out to this week.
  • What does a watchman see? Show a picture of a watchtower. Ask what a watchman can see that people below cannot. Connect to the living prophet’s recent counsel and to the youth’s own chances to warn a friend kindly.
  • “How should we then live?” Hand out index cards. Students anonymously write a question someone might ask when they feel weighed down by mistakes. Answer a few together using Ezekiel 33:11, 15–16 and 36:26.

Tie to Jesus Christ

Ezekiel 34:11–16, 23 · John 10:11–16

He is the Good Shepherd

Every job the false shepherds skipped—strengthening, healing, binding up, bringing back, seeking—Jesus does Himself (Luke 15:4–7). “My servant David” points to the Messiah, the promised Son of David (Luke 1:32), who gathers every flock into one fold.

Ezekiel 36:26 · Mosiah 5:7

He gives the new heart

We can’t carve our own stony heart into a new one. Through His Atonement, Christ changes us so we become His spiritually begotten sons and daughters.

Ezekiel 37:12–14 · John 11:25

He opens the graves

“I am the resurrection, and the life.” Because Jesus rose, the valley of dry bones is not just a picture of Israel’s hope—it is a promise for every person who has ever died (1 Corinthians 15:22).

Ezekiel 47:1–12 · John 4:14; Revelation 22:1–2

He is the source of living water

Jesus offered “living water” that becomes “a well of water springing up into everlasting life.” John’s vision repeats Ezekiel’s: a river from God’s throne and a tree whose leaves heal the nations.

Ezekiel 1:26–28; 43:2 · D&C 110:2–4

His glory comes to His people

Ezekiel saw a figure like a man on the throne and heard a voice “like a noise of many waters.” In the Kirtland Temple in 1836, Joseph Smith and Oliver Cowdery saw the resurrected Lord, whose voice was “as the sound of the rushing of great waters.” The God Ezekiel saw in exile is the Savior who still comes to His temples.
