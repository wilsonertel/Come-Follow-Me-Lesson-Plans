The History: Building the Salt Lake Temple

A people with a temple-shaped grief. The Saints who entered the Salt Lake Valley in July 1847 were not new to temple building — they were new to losing temples. They had built and dedicated the Kirtland Temple in 1836 and then abandoned Ohio. They had poured five years and enormous sacrifice into the Nauvoo Temple, held a rushed season of ordinances there in the winter of 1845–46, and walked away as the wagons rolled west. They arrived in the West carrying the memory of a house of God they no longer had.

Four days in. The main pioneer company reached the valley on July 24, 1847. On July 28 — four days later, before anyone had a real house — Brigham Young walked the sagebrush flat with members of the Quorum of the Twelve, struck the ground with his cane, and said in substance, "Here we will build the temple of our God." Wilford Woodruff drove a stake into the hole. That evening the ten-acre block was marked out, and the whole city was laid out to grow outward from it: the temple first, the streets afterward. In that same first week, Elder George A. Smith preached that a temple in these mountains would fulfill Isaiah's promise that "the mountain of the Lord's house shall be established in the top of the mountains" (Isaiah 2:2).

A very poor people and a very large plan. The Saints voted in general conference in April 1851 to build. Ground was broken February 14, 1853 — Heber C. Kimball dedicated the site, Brigham Young turned the first shovel, and the crowd sang "Auld Lang Syne." The cornerstones were laid April 6, 1853. Truman O. Angell, a carpenter who became the Church architect, drew the plans under Brigham Young's direction, with William Ward assisting. From the start the exterior was designed to preach: six towers instead of Nauvoo's one, and stones carved as earthstones, moonstones, sunstones, and starstones.

"I scarcely ever say much about revelations, or visions, but suffice it to say, five years ago last July I was here, and saw in the Spirit the Temple not ten feet from where we have laid the Chief Corner Stone."Brigham Young · April 6, 1853 · Journal of Discourses 1:133

The stone problem. The footings were first laid in red sandstone from Red Butte Canyon, close at hand. The walls were to be granite (technically quartz monzonite) from Little Cottonwood Canyon, about twenty miles southeast. There was no railroad. Ox teams hauled single blocks across the valley on a round trip that could take four days — longer in bad weather. Wards and priesthood quorums took turns supplying stonecutters, teamsters, and provisions as a form of labor tithing. Passersby on Temple Square described the constant clatter of chisels on stone as a kind of music.

Why granite?

After the sandstone footings failed, Heber C. Kimball put the question to the Saints directly: should the temple be built of Red Butte stone, adobe, or "the best materials that can be obtained in the mountains of North America"? They chose the hardest, heaviest, most inconvenient option available. Brigham Young's standard was blunt: he wanted a temple "built in a manner that it will endure through the Millennium." That single decision added years to the project — and is a large part of why the building is still standing after a 2020 earthquake and a full seismic renovation.

Opposition from outside. In 1857–58 a U.S. Army expedition was sent to Utah on false reports of rebellion. Rather than risk the temple, the Saints buried the foundation and plowed the block so it looked like an ordinary field, then evacuated the city southward. Johnston's Army marched through an empty Salt Lake City in June 1858. Decades later, in the 1880s, federal antipolygamy legislation (the Edmunds–Tucker Act) authorized seizure of Church property and directly threatened the temples themselves. President Wilford Woodruff issued the Manifesto in 1890; the pressure eased, and the Saints sprinted for the finish.

Opposition from within the project. When the buried foundation was uncovered around 1860–62, the sandstone was found to be cracked and the footing defective. Brigham Young is remembered as sitting down on one of the ruined stones and saying he would not move from the spot until he knew what to do. The answer was to tear out everything already built and start the footings over in granite, roughly sixteen feet thick. Fourteen years after groundbreaking — in 1867 — the walls finally rose above ground level.

Three prophets and a generation of workmen. Brigham Young died in 1877 with the walls only about twenty feet above the ground; back in 1853 he had told the Saints he did not know whether he would see it finished and did not much care, so long as he was doing what God required that day. John Taylor led during the hardest legal years. Wilford Woodruff finished it. Truman Angell died in 1887, before completion; Joseph Don Carlos Young — Brigham's son and a trained engineer — completed the spires and interior, adding a steel frame, electric lighting, steam heating, and elevators. Interior plans were revised along the way as the St. George (1877), Logan (1884), and Manti (1888) temples taught the Church how temple ordinances actually needed to flow.

One man's story — John Rowe Moyle

A Cornish stonemason who joined the Church in England, crossed the plains with the first handcart company in 1856, and settled in Alpine, about twenty-two miles from Temple Square. Called as a stonecutter on the temple, he left home around 2:00 a.m. Monday, walked six hours to be at his post by eight, worked the week, and walked home Friday night, arriving near midnight — every week, for years. A cow kicked him and shattered his leg; it was amputated below the knee with a bucksaw. When it healed, he carved himself a wooden leg with a hinged ankle, taught himself to walk on it, and went back to the temple. His hands carved "Holiness to the Lord, the House of the Lord" on the east face. He died in 1889 — four years before the dedication.

The finish. The exterior was completed in 1892. On April 6 of that year, tens of thousands of Saints packed Temple Square for the capstone ceremony, gave the Hosanna Shout — the first time they were instructed to wave white handkerchiefs as they did it — and watched a roughly twelve-foot statue of the angel Moroni, sculpted by Cyrus Dallin, set on the east center spire. President Woodruff then asked that the interior be finished within one year. Crews worked at speed; the annex and interior were completed the day before the dedication. On April 6, 1893 — forty years to the day from the laying of the cornerstone — President Wilford Woodruff offered the dedicatory prayer. Sessions ran twice a day for nearly three weeks, including sessions for children, so that every Latter-day Saint who wanted to be there could be.

Timeline: Forty Years in Stone

From a cane in the dirt to a golden statue on the spire — with the setbacks left in.

1847
July 24 · July 28

The valley, then the site

The pioneer company arrives July 24. Four days later Brigham Young marks the temple spot with his cane; Wilford Woodruff drives a stake. The ten-acre block is surveyed that evening and the city is platted around it.

1851
April conference

The Saints vote to build

In general conference the members sustain the decision to build a temple — four years of survival farming after arriving in a desert basin.

1853
Feb. 14 · April 6

Ground broken; cornerstones laid

Thousands stand in the February cold; the crowd sings "Auld Lang Syne." On April 6 the cornerstones are laid, and Brigham Young tells the congregation he had already seen the temple in vision, and that it would have six towers.

1853
–57
The slow years

Sandstone footings, ox teams, four-day hauls

Footings go in with red sandstone from Red Butte Canyon. Granite blocks are dragged from Little Cottonwood Canyon by ox team — up to four days for a single stone. Crop failures and hard winters repeatedly pull men off the project.

1858
The Utah War

The temple is buried

With a U.S. Army column approaching, the Saints cover the foundation with dirt and plow the block so it looks like a field, then evacuate south. Johnston's Army marches through a deserted city in June. The site stays hidden until about 1860.

1861
–62
The crisis

Cracked stone, and a decision to start over

When the foundation is uncovered, the sandstone is cracked and the footing defective. Brigham Young orders every compromised stone removed and replaced with granite footings roughly sixteen feet thick. Years of finished work are undone on purpose.

1867
Year 14

The walls finally clear the ground

Fourteen years after groundbreaking, there is at last something above ground level to look at.

1868
–73
Railroad detour

Delayed by a railroad, then rescued by one

Brigham Young pulls temple workers off to help build the transcontinental railroad, finished May 1869. In 1873 a rail spur reaches the quarry. Delivery of a granite block drops from about four days by ox team to roughly one hour by steam engine.

1877
Succession

Brigham Young dies with walls twenty feet high

He dedicates the St. George Temple in January and dies in August, never seeing the Salt Lake Temple rise past about twenty feet. John Taylor, then Wilford Woodruff, carry it on. Logan (1884) and Manti (1888) are completed first, and what is learned there reshapes the Salt Lake interior plans.

1887
–90
Legal siege

The government threatens to take the temples

Federal antipolygamy law authorizes seizure of Church property; the temples themselves are at risk. In 1890 President Woodruff issues the Manifesto. Pressure eases, and work accelerates toward completion.

1892
April 6

The capstone and the shout

The exterior is finished. Tens of thousands gather; the Hosanna Shout is given with white handkerchiefs waved for the first time; Cyrus Dallin's twelve-foot angel Moroni is placed on the east center spire. President Woodruff asks that the inside be done in one year.

1893
April 6 · and 20 days after

Dedicated — forty years to the day

The interior is finished the afternoon before. President Wilford Woodruff offers the dedicatory prayer at the first session. Sessions run twice daily for nearly three weeks, including sessions for children. Brigham Young, Truman Angell, and John Moyle are all long dead. The building they never saw finished is the one the Church is still using today.

Numbers worth putting on the board

46 years from Brigham Young's cane to the dedication. 40 years from cornerstone to dedication, to the day. 14 years before the walls cleared the ground. 20 miles from quarry to temple block. 4 days to move one stone by ox — 1 hour by rail. 3 Church presidents, 2 chief architects, and an unknown number of stonecutters who died before the doors opened.

1. Help from an unexpected quarter
Ezra 1:1–4; 6:8–12

Cyrus — a Persian king who did not worship Jehovah — is stirred up by the Lord to fund the temple out of the royal treasury. Later Darius reaffirms the order and pays for it.

Salt Lake, 1858 & 1869

Thomas L. Kane, a non-member friend of the Saints, traveled at his own expense to mediate the Utah War and helped avert bloodshed. And the transcontinental railroad — built by outsiders for money, not religion — ended up cutting a four-day haul to one hour.

2. Weeping and shouting at the same moment
Ezra 3:10–13

When the foundation was laid, the young shouted for joy and the old men who had seen Solomon's temple wept aloud — and no one could tell the noises apart.

Feb. 14, 1853

At the groundbreaking the Saints sang "Auld Lang Syne" — a song about old friends and days gone by. They had buried Joseph Smith, lost Kirtland, and left Nauvoo's temple behind. The new start carried the old grief inside it.

3. Opposition that actually works — for a while
Ezra 4:4–24

Adversaries "weakened the hands" of the builders, hired counselors against them, and finally got a royal order to stop. The work ceased until the second year of Darius — about sixteen lost years.

1858 & the 1880s

The foundation was buried and disguised as a plowed field so an approaching army wouldn't find it. Thirty years later, federal law authorized seizing Church property, temples included.

4. Prophets restart what discouragement stopped
Ezra 5:1–2; Haggai 1:2–8

Haggai and Zechariah prophesied, and Zerubbabel and Jeshua "rose up" and began building again. Haggai's rebuke: the people had gotten comfortable in their own paneled houses while the Lord's house lay waste.

About 1862

Facing cracked footings, Brigham Young is remembered as sitting down on the ruined stone and refusing to move until he knew what to do — then ordering every bad stone pulled and replaced with granite.

5. Building with one hand, defending with the other
Nehemiah 4:16–18

Half the men worked while half held spears; the builders wore a sword at the side while laying stone, with a trumpeter beside Nehemiah to sound an alarm.

1850s–60s

The same men who cut temple granite were also breaking desert ground, forting up against conflict, and evacuating their families south. John Moyle built a fort in Alpine during the army's approach — and still went back to the temple.

6. "I cannot come down"
Nehemiah 6:1–4

Enemies invited Nehemiah to a meeting in the plain of Ono, meaning to harm him. His answer four times over: "I am doing a great work, so that I cannot come down."

John Rowe Moyle

Twenty-two miles on foot each way, every week. Then a shattered leg, an amputation with a bucksaw, a hand-carved wooden leg — and back to the same twenty-two miles and the same chisel.

7. Finished by people who weren't there at the start
Ezra 3:12; 6:15

The men who wept at the foundation remembered a temple most of the builders had never seen. Many who returned from Babylon in 538 BC did not live to see the dedication in 515 BC.

1853 → 1893

Brigham Young, Truman Angell, and John Moyle all died before the doors opened. Three Church presidents and two chief architects carried it. Children born the year of the groundbreaking were forty at the dedication.

8. A dedication the whole people attends
Ezra 6:16–22; Nehemiah 8:1–12

Israel kept the dedication with joy, offered sacrifice, and kept the Passover. Later the whole congregation — men, women, and "all that could hear with understanding" — gathered while Ezra read the law.

April 1893

Sessions twice a day for nearly three weeks, including sessions just for children, specifically so that ordinary Saints — not just leaders — could be inside for the dedication of the house they had paid for and built.

Honest differences worth naming

The parallels are strong but not identical. Cyrus funded the Jerusalem temple; the U.S. government mostly obstructed the Salt Lake Temple. Zerubbabel's temple was rebuilt on the ruins of Solomon's, deliberately more modest, and the ark of the covenant was never returned — the Holy of Holies stood empty. The Salt Lake Temple was built larger and more ornate than anything the Saints had attempted. And the Jerusalem temple's ordinances were the Mosaic sacrificial system; the Salt Lake Temple's are the endowment and sealing ordinances of the restored gospel. Same pattern of faith and opposition; different dispensations, different covenants.
