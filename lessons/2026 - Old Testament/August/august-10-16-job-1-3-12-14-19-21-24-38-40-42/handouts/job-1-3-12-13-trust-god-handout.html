1

Historical Summary of the Period

A book with no address. Every Old Testament book you have studied so far is anchored in time by a pharaoh, a king, an exile, or a decree. Job has none. No Israel, no Moses, no law, no tabernacle, no exodus, no dateable event anywhere in forty-two chapters. That silence is deliberate, and it tells you what kind of book this is: the message is meant to travel to anyone, anywhere.

Where: the land of Uz. Job is “the greatest of all the men of the east” (Job 1:3). Uz appears only twice more in scripture, both times linked with Edom, the country of Esau’s descendants southeast of the Dead Sea (Jeremiah 25:20–21; Lamentations 4:21). The internal clues fit that neighborhood: his friend Eliphaz is a Temanite, and Teman was an Edomite site near what later became Petra; Job lives at the desert’s edge (1:19) on land fertile enough to graze thousands of animals; and he is within raiding range of both Sabeans from southern Arabia and Chaldeans from lower Mesopotamia (1:15, 17). Some scholars propose the Wadi Sirhan depression in northern Arabia, which satisfies all of these at once. Every proposal agrees on one thing: Uz is outside Canaan. Job is almost certainly not an Israelite.

When: probably the patriarchal era, roughly the second millennium BC. Five details point toward the world of Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob:

  • Wealth counted in livestock (Job 1:3; 42:12), exactly how Genesis measures Abraham and Jacob (Genesis 12:16; 13:2; 30:43).
  • Job serves as priest for his own family, offering sacrifices for his children (Job 1:5)—a pre-Aaronic pattern that faded once nations built formal priesthoods.
  • The qesitah, the unit of money Job’s relatives bring him (Job 42:11), appears elsewhere in scripture only around Jacob (Genesis 33:19; Joshua 24:32).
  • A patriarch-length lifespan: Job lives 140 more years and sees four generations (Job 42:16).
  • Sabeans and Chaldeans as raiding nomads rather than the settled kingdoms they later became (Job 1:15, 17).

None of this is airtight, and honest scholarship says so—the book names no historical event. But it explains why Job feels so ancient.

The idea Job is arguing against. The ancient Near East ran on what scholars call the retribution principle: the righteous prosper, the wicked suffer, and therefore anyone suffering must have done something to earn it. That assumption is the engine of this entire book. It is what Eliphaz, Bildad, and Zophar believe, it is why they slide from comfort into accusation, and it is what the Lord finally rebukes them for (Job 42:7–9). Job’s friends are not villains. They are ordinary people defending God with bad theology.

Concrete anchor · the “Babylonian Job”

Job was not the only ancient text asking this question. Cuneiform tablets recovered from the library of the Assyrian king Ashurbanipal at Nineveh preserve a poem called Ludlul bêl nêmeqi, “I will praise the lord of wisdom,” nicknamed the Babylonian Job. A wealthy official named Shubshi-meshre-Shakkan loses status, health, and friends, cannot discover what he did wrong, and is eventually restored by the god Marduk. Related texts include the Babylonian Theodicy, the Sumerian A Man and His God, and the Canaanite story of King Keret, who—like Job—loses seven sons.

The differences are where Job gets its power. In the Mesopotamian poems the sufferer is never truly blameless, the deity is inscrutable, and the god never shows up to speak. In Job the man is upright, the question is pressed all the way down, and God answers in person. Job is not a copy of those texts. It is the answer to them.

The customs behind chapters 1–2. Several details read as strange until you know the culture. Tearing the robe and shaving the head are formal, public mourning acts, not a breakdown (1:20). Sitting among ashes and scraping sores with a shard of pottery (2:8) puts Job on the ash heap outside town—the village refuse dump, the last place a wealthy man would ever be found. And the three friends sitting with him seven days without a word (2:13) is not awkwardness; it is proper mourning practice, and it is the single best thing they do in the whole book. Their trouble begins the moment they open their mouths.

Was Job a real person? The poetic form has led some to wonder whether he is a literary figure. Latter-day Saints have a plain reason for confidence: scripture and modern prophets treat him as an actual man. Ezekiel names Job with Noah and Daniel as a byword for righteousness (Ezekiel 14:14, 20), James points to his endurance (James 5:11), and the Lord invokes him by name to Joseph Smith in Liberty Jail (Doctrine and Covenants 121:10).

Passage Summary

Chapters 1–2 are prose. Chapter 3 is where the poetry begins—and where the book stops being a story about a man and becomes an argument about God.

1
Job 1:1–5

A man worth describing

Job is “perfect and upright,” a Hebrew phrase meaning blameless, complete, or whole—not sinless. Seven sons, three daughters, enormous herds. Notice his instinct: when his children hold feasts he offers sacrifices for them just in case. Job’s religion is proactive, not reactive.

2
Job 1:6–12

The question that runs the book

In a heavenly council an accuser raises a challenge: Job only worships because he has been paid well. Take the blessings away and he will curse God. Everything that follows is a test of that claim—which means the book’s real question is not “why do good people suffer?” but why do you serve God?

3
Job 1:13–19

Four messengers, one day

Oxen and donkeys raided by Sabeans. Sheep and servants burned. Camels taken by Chaldeans. Then a wind collapses the house where all ten children were gathered. Each messenger arrives before the previous one finishes speaking. The pacing is the point: there is no room to breathe.

4
Job 1:20–22

He falls down and worships

Job tears his robe, shaves his head—and worships. “The Lord gave, and the Lord hath taken away” (1:21). The text is emphatic that in all of this he did not sin or charge God foolishly. Grief and faith are happening in the same body at the same time.

5
Job 2:1–8

Skin for skin

The accuser presses again: take his health and he will break. Job is struck with painful sores from head to foot and ends up on the ash heap with a shard of pottery. He has now lost property, children, and body.

6
Job 2:9–10

The hardest sentence in the chapter

His wife tells him to curse God and die. Job’s answer is one of the most remarkable lines in scripture: shall we receive good at God’s hand and not adversity? He does not say the adversity is good. He says it is not disqualifying.

7
Job 2:11–13

Three friends do it right

Eliphaz, Bildad, and Zophar travel from their own countries to comfort him, do not recognize him, weep, and sit with him on the ground for seven days and seven nights without saying a word. Everything they do here is correct. Everything they say afterward is wrong.

8
Job 3

Job breaks

The poetry begins. Job curses the day he was born and wishes he had never drawn breath. This is essential for teaching: lament is not apostasy. Job says devastating things about his own life—but never once curses God, which is exactly what the accuser predicted. Moses, Jeremiah, and Jonah all voice the same anguish.

9
Job 12

Job knows more theology than his friends

He opens with heavy sarcasm: no doubt they are the people, and wisdom will die when they do (12:2). Then he out-preaches them—in God’s hand is the life of every living thing (12:10); with Him are wisdom and strength (12:13, 16). Job’s crisis is not a shortage of belief. He believes all of it. That is what makes it hurt.

10
Job 13:1–12

“Physicians of no value”

Job accuses his friends of lying on God’s behalf—of speaking wickedly for God and accepting His person out of partiality (13:7–8). He warns that God will not thank them for defending Him dishonestly. Chapter 42 proves him right.

11
Job 13:13–28

The verse the week is named after

“Though he slay me, yet will I trust in him” (13:15)—and in the same breath, “but I will maintain mine own ways before him.” Job’s trust is not passive. He intends to keep trusting and keep asking. That combination is the entire lesson.

Teacher’s note · how to read Job 1–2

Someone will ask whether God really made a bet with Satan. The Come, Follow Me outline addresses this directly: these opening chapters emphasize, in a poetic way, Satan’s role as our adversary and accuser—they are not describing an actual transaction between God and Satan. The Hebrew word rendered “Satan” simply means adversary or accuser, and in Hebrew it carries the definite article: the accuser, a role in the scene. The Joseph Smith Translation changes “sons of God” to “children of God” in Job 1:6. Give students two anchors: God has power over Satan, and God does only what benefits His children (2 Nephi 26:24).

Teacher’s note · be fair to Job’s wife

Job’s wife gets one line and centuries of criticism. Remember what the text says: she buried the same ten children. She is watching her husband die slowly on a garbage heap. Her words in 2:9 are the sound of a woman at the end of her endurance, not a portrait of a wicked person. Job does not call her wicked either—he says she is speaking as one of the foolish women speak, which in Hebrew wisdom vocabulary means speaking without thinking it through. This is worth naming out loud for a class of thirteen-year-olds, who will otherwise walk away with a cartoon.

Comparison to the Book of Mormon

The prologue raises the question of motive, and chapters 3 and 12–13 show what honest faith sounds like under pressure. The Book of Mormon speaks to both.

Job 1:9–11

The accuser’s charge: Job serves God because it pays. Remove the profit and the worship stops.

Mosiah 2:20–24

King Benjamin points out that God immediately blesses us for every act of obedience, so we remain unprofitable servants—we can never get ahead of Him.

Teaches: You cannot serve God as a transaction, because the ledger never balances. Obedience has to become gratitude or it collapses under pressure.
Job 1:20–22

Job tears his robe, shaves his head, falls to the ground—and worships in the same motion as his grief.

Alma 34:40–41

Amulek counsels the poor among the Zoramites to bear afflictions with patience, not revile those who cast them out, and hold a firm hope.

Teaches: Worship is a decision available in the moment of loss, not only after the loss makes sense.
Job 2:9–10

“Shall we receive good at the hand of God, and shall we not receive evil?”

2 Nephi 2:11–13

Lehi teaches that opposition in all things is built into mortality by design; without it, righteousness, happiness, and existence itself collapse.

Teaches: Opposition is a feature of the plan, not a malfunction and not a verdict on your worthiness.
Job 2:11–13

Seven days of silence beside a suffering friend—the one thing the three friends get completely right.

Mosiah 18:8–10

The baptismal covenant at the Waters of Mormon: bear one another’s burdens, mourn with those that mourn, comfort those that stand in need of comfort.

Teaches: Presence comes before explanation. Mourning with people is a covenant obligation; fixing them is not.
Job 3:1–16

Job curses the day of his birth and wishes he had died at birth—raw, unfiltered anguish, spoken to God rather than about Him.

2 Nephi 4:17–35

Nephi’s psalm: “O wretched man that I am… my heart sorroweth,” followed immediately by a declaration of trust in the God who has upheld him.

Teaches: Scripture makes room for honest despair. Saying it out loud to God is not the opposite of faith—it is a form of it.
Job 3:20–26

Job asks why light is given to a man in misery, and says the thing he greatly feared has come upon him.

Alma 36:12–20

Alma the Younger describes three days racked with torment, wishing for extinction—until he remembers Jesus Christ and the same memory that tormented him becomes his joy.

Teaches: The darkest point of an account is often the doorway, not the ending. Students who feel like Job 3 need to know that.
Job 12:10, 13, 16

Job recites what he knows about God—that all life is in His hand, and that wisdom and strength belong to Him—while he is still on the ash heap.

Mosiah 4:9

Believe in God; believe that He is, that He created all things, that He has all wisdom and all power, both in heaven and in earth.

Teaches: The theology you build before a crisis is the theology you draw on during one. Job is quoting himself back to himself.
Job 13:15

“Though he slay me, yet will I trust in him”—trust with no promise of rescue attached to it.

Alma 24:20–23

The Anti-Nephi-Lehies lie down and let themselves be killed rather than break a covenant, praising God as they die.

Teaches: Real trust is not certainty that God will fix it. It is loyalty that does not depend on being fixed.

Tie to Come, Follow Me

This week’s outline: “Yet Will I Trust in Him”—Job 1–3; 12–14; 19; 21–24; 38–40; 42, August 10–16, 2026. This handout covers the manual’s first principle. The manual frames the week around an honest observation: it is natural to wonder why bad things happen to good people, and why good things happen to bad ones. Its summary line is worth writing on the board—in the book of Job, faith is questioned and tested but never abandoned; questions can coexist with faith.

What the manual asks students to do with Job 1–3; 12–13

  • Start with the accuser’s question, not Job’s. Read what Satan says about Job (1:9–11; 2:4–5), then ask, in the manual’s words, “Why do I remain faithful to God?” and why obeying solely for the reason the adversary suggested would be dangerous.
  • Look at how Job actually responded (1:20–22; 2:9–10). The manual asks simply: what impresses you about these responses?
  • Weigh the friends’ theory. Job’s friends suggested God was punishing him (Job 4–5; 8; 11). What problems do you see with that idea?
  • Read Job’s answer in chapters 12–13. What did Job know about God that enabled him to keep trusting? And—the personal turn the manual wants—what do you know about God that helps you face challenges?
  • Scripture Helps for this section answer why Job cursed the day of his birth, what we know about Job, and whether the Lord really conversed with Satan. All three are covered in Sections 1 and 2 above.
Genre note the manual sets up first

Immediately before this week, the manual inserts “Thoughts to Keep in Mind: Reading Poetry in the Old Testament.” From Genesis through Esther the Old Testament is mostly story; from Job forward it is mostly poetry. Job opens and closes in prose—the prologue in 1–2 and the epilogue in 42—and everything between is Hebrew verse. Hebrew poetry does not rhyme; its signature move is parallelism, saying a thing twice with a deliberate variation. Job belongs to what scholars call wisdom literature, with Proverbs and Ecclesiastes: writing that wrestles with big questions rather than narrating events. A student who reads chapter 3 like a newspaper report will conclude Job lost his testimony. A student who reads it like a poem will hear grief.

Teaching ideas for youth Sunday School (ages 12–13)

  • Open with the accuser’s question in plain English. Write on the board: “Would you still keep the commandments if it made your life harder instead of easier?” Let them answer honestly before they know that is the exact charge in Job 1:9–11. It reframes the book from “why do bad things happen” to “why do you follow God.”
  • Four messengers, read aloud fast. Assign four students verses 1:14–19 and have them read back-to-back with no pause. The compression is the whole emotional point and it is lost in silent reading.
  • The silence experiment. Have the class sit silently for sixty seconds, then point out the friends did that for seven days—and that it was the best thing they did. Ask what makes silence hard, and when a hurting friend needs it.
  • Sort the statements. Give six short statements—three from Job (1:21; 2:10; 13:15), three from his friends (4:7–8; 8:6; 11:6)—without labels, and have them guess who said what. Then reveal that God rebuked the second group. It lands harder than a lecture on bad comfort.
  • Name lament out loud. Read Job 3 and ask directly: is Job losing his testimony here? Walk them to the answer—he says terrible things about his own life and never once curses God. Thirteen-year-olds need permission to be sad without thinking they are failing.
  • Be ready for a real one. Someone in your class may be carrying something heavy right now. Have the Teacher’s Notes above ready, and be willing to say plainly that the book never answers why—and that not knowing why is not the same as not being loved.
  • Close on 13:15. Write the whole verse, including the second half: but I will maintain mine own ways before him. Ask what it means that Job kept trusting and kept asking. That is the thesis of the entire week.

Tie to Jesus Christ

The accuser opens this section by questioning a man’s motives. The Savior is the answer to that—in more ways than one.

Accuser and Advocate

The Hebrew word behind “Satan” means accuser—and that is precisely the role played in Job 1–2: someone stands up and argues that a man’s righteousness is fake. Scripture gives that figure a permanent counterpart. Jesus Christ is our Advocate with the Father, who stands up and argues the other way: not that we are flawless, but that He suffered and died for us and therefore claims us. Every accusation in this book has an answer, and the answer is a Person.Job 1:9–11 · D&C 45:3–5 · 1 John 2:1 · Revelation 12:10

“Doth Job fear God for nought?”

The accuser’s theory is that no one would love God without payment. Gethsemane and Calvary are the permanent refutation—not of Job’s loyalty but of the theory itself. The Savior obeyed the Father when obedience cost Him everything and returned nothing in the moment. Job’s worship on the ash heap is a small echo of something the Savior would do completely.Job 1:9 · Luke 22:42 · Matthew 26:39 · John 6:38

“Though he slay me”—and He was

Job’s most famous line in this section is hypothetical: even if He kills me, I will trust Him. The Savior lived the sentence without the “if.” On the cross, having quoted a psalm of abandonment, He still committed His spirit into the Father’s hands. Whatever Job meant by trusting through death, Christ demonstrated it.Job 13:15 · Luke 23:46 · Psalm 22:1 · Mark 15:34

“In whose hand is the soul of every living thing”

The God Job describes in chapter 12—holding the life of every creature, possessing all wisdom and strength—is identified in latter-day scripture as Jehovah, Jesus Christ, acting under the Father. The Being Job clung to in the dark was the same Being who would come down, take a body, and endure worse than Job did.Job 12:10, 13 · John 1:1–3 · Mosiah 3:8 · 3 Nephi 9:15

He does not merely permit suffering—He enters it

Job’s friends offered theories from a safe distance. Alma prophesied that the Savior would go forth suffering pains, afflictions, and temptations of every kind, taking upon Himself our sicknesses so He would know according to the flesh how to succor us. That is the difference between an explanation and a Redeemer. Job wanted the first and was given the second.Alma 7:11–13 · Hebrews 4:15 · D&C 122:8 · Isaiah 53:3–4
