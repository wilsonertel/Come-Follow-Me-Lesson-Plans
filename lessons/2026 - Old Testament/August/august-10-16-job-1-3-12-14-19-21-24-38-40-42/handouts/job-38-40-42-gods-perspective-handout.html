1

Historical Summary of the Period

God speaks out of a storm. When the Lord finally answers, He does it from a whirlwind (38:1). Storm imagery for divine appearance is deeply embedded in the ancient Near East, and the Old Testament uses it repeatedly—God comes in cloud, wind, thunder, and fire at Sinai, to Elijah at Horeb, and in Ezekiel’s opening vision. For Job the setting is pointed: the last time wind appeared in this book, it collapsed the house and killed his children (1:19). The same element that took everything from him is now the medium through which God addresses him personally.

What kind of speech this is. God’s answer is a sustained tour of the created world—the sea and the dawn, snow and hail and lightning, the constellations, and then a long procession of wild animals: lioness, raven, mountain goat, wild donkey, wild ox, ostrich, war horse, hawk, eagle. Scholars have compared this to an old scribal genre found across Egypt and Mesopotamia in which the world was catalogued in ordered lists of sky, earth, waters, creatures, and occupations—the Egyptian onomastica are the best-known examples. The comparison is debated and Job is clearly doing something the lists never do: his catalog is poetry, it is addressed to a person, and every item arrives as a question. Same raw material, entirely different purpose. The lists inventory the world; God is showing a man how large it is.

Concrete anchor · what the animals have in common

Look at the creatures God selects. Not one of them is domesticated or useful to Job. The wild donkey that scorns the city and laughs at the driver. The wild ox that will never plow your field. The ostrich, described as leaving her eggs in the dirt and lacking sense—and yet she outruns the horse. The eagle nesting on a crag where no one can reach her. Then behemoth and leviathan, which no one can capture at all.

The pattern is the argument. God runs an entire wild economy that has nothing to do with human usefulness, feeds animals Job has never seen, and sends rain on wilderness where no man lives (38:26). The point is not you are small. It is the world is not organized around your needs, and it is still being cared for. That is a far kinder message, and it is the one Job actually receives.

Behemoth and leviathan. These two creatures close the Lord’s speech. The Hebrew behemoth is the plural form of “beast” and describes a massive land animal, possibly a poetic hippopotamus. Leviathan is a great sea creature—perhaps a crocodile, and the name is also known from ancient Canaanite texts describing a monstrous sea serpent. What matters is not the zoology but the function: they represent forces of nature no human being can tame, and the Lord invokes them to show the power He holds over all of creation.

Job 38:7 and the premortal council. One verse in this section opens a door no other Old Testament passage does. The Lord refers to a moment when the morning stars sang together and all the sons of God shouted for joy—a scene from before the earth existed. Latter-day Saints understand this as the Council in Heaven, where Heavenly Father presented His plan. President Thomas S. Monson taught, in substance, that we lived before mortal birth and were doubtless among those who shouted for joy at the chance to come to this challenging but necessary existence. That reframes the entire book: Job is being reminded that he agreed to a plan that included this.

The epilogue’s legal language. Job is restored double (42:10). In Israelite law, double is the language of restitution—a thief who is caught restores twofold to the person he wronged. Whatever else the epilogue is doing, it is using the vocabulary of a wronged party being made whole rather than a lucky man receiving a bonus. Note too what is not doubled: Job receives ten more children, not twenty. The most natural reading, and the one that fits Restoration doctrine, is that the first ten were never actually lost.

The daughters. Easy to skip and worth ten seconds in class. Job’s three daughters are named—Jemima, Kezia, and Keren-happuch—while the seven sons are not, and they are given an inheritance among their brothers (42:15). Under the inheritance provisions later given in Israel, daughters received a father’s inheritance when there were no sons (Numbers 27:1–8). Job has seven sons and gives his daughters an inheritance anyway. The book ends by quietly doing something unusually generous.

Passage Summary

Thirty-five chapters of argument end the moment God opens His mouth—and He never once addresses the topic everyone was arguing about.

1
Job 38:1–3

“Who is this that darkeneth counsel?”

The Lord answers out of the whirlwind and opens by telling Job to gird up his loins like a man—I will demand of thee, and answer thou me. Notice immediately who is asking the questions now. Job came prepared to present a case; he is put on the stand instead.

2
Job 38:4–7

“Where wast thou?”

Where were you when I laid the foundations of the earth, when the morning stars sang together and all the sons of God shouted for joy? This is the premortal council, and it is the theological center of the whole speech: Job existed then, and he was glad about this plan.

3
Job 38:8–21

Sea, dawn, and the gates of death

Who shut up the sea with doors and said, this far and no farther? Have you commanded the morning? Have the gates of death been opened to you? Have you walked in the search of the depth? God moves through the boundaries of creation, all of them set by someone else.

4
Job 38:22–38

The storehouses

Snow and hail in treasuries, rain on the wilderness where no man is, the sweet influences of the Pleiades and the bands of Orion, the ordinances of heaven. Verse 26 is the quiet key: God waters ground nobody lives on. Care is not contingent on usefulness.

5
Job 38:39–39:30

The wild things

A procession of untamed creatures: hunting lioness, raven’s chicks crying, wild goats calving in the rocks, the free donkey laughing at the city, the wild ox that will never serve you, the strange ostrich, the war horse that mocks at fear, the hawk, the eagle on the crag. Job knows none of their business, and their business goes on.

6
Job 40:1–5

Job’s first answer

“Behold, I am vile; what shall I answer thee? I will lay mine hand upon my mouth.” He has spoken once, twice—he will not go further. This is submission, but not yet understanding. God is not finished.

7
Job 40:6–14

“Wilt thou also disannul my judgment?”

The second speech gets sharper: will you condemn Me so that you can be righteous? Then dress yourself in majesty, bring down the proud, and tread the wicked into the dust—and I will confess that your own arm can save you. It is challenge, not cruelty. Job had claimed he could run the case better.

8
Job 40:15–24

Behemoth

Bones like brass, strength in his loins, a tail like a cedar; he lies under the shady trees and is unbothered when the river rages. No one takes him by force. God made him, and God is unimpressed.

9
Job 41

Leviathan

An entire chapter on a creature nobody can hook, tame, or make a pet of—scales shut up together, breath kindling coals, no fear on earth. If Job cannot handle a leviathan, the argument runs, perhaps managing the moral government of the universe is out of reach too.

10
Job 42:1–6

From hearing to seeing

Job concedes: I know You can do everything; I uttered what I did not understand, things too wonderful for me. Then the pivot the whole book has been driving at—I have heard of thee by the hearing of the ear: but now mine eye seeth thee. He does not confess the sins his friends invented. He repents of having accused God of injustice.

11
Job 42:7–9

The verdict, and it is not what anyone expects

The Lord’s anger burns against Eliphaz, Bildad, and Zophar—because they have not spoken of Him the thing that is right, as my servant Job hath. The man who spent thirty chapters complaining is vindicated; the men who defended God are told to bring sacrifices and have Job pray for them.

12
Job 42:10–17

Restored

Note the sequence carefully: the Lord turned Job’s captivity when he prayed for his friends. Family and neighbors come back, each bringing a piece of money and an earring. He receives double the livestock and ten more children. His three daughters are named and given an inheritance among their brothers. He lives 140 more years and sees four generations.

Translation note · Job 42:6

The King James text reads “I abhor myself,” but “myself” is italicized, meaning it is not in the Hebrew—translators supplied it to make sense of a compressed line. The Hebrew verb can mean to renounce or reject rather than to loathe. Job may well be saying, I take it back, and I repent in dust and ashes. That is a meaningfully different ending: not a broken man hating himself, but an honest man withdrawing a charge he should not have made. President Henry B. Eyring has taught, in substance, that after Job repented of calling God unfair he was permitted to see his trials in a higher and holier way. Correct this if a student reads self-hatred into the verse.

Comparison to the Book of Mormon

The Lord’s speech touches doctrines the Old Testament barely mentions elsewhere—premortal existence, foreordination, God’s purposes in affliction. The Book of Mormon fills each of them in.

Job 38:7

The morning stars sang together and all the sons of God shouted for joy—a scene from before the earth was.

Alma 13:3

Priests were called and prepared from the foundation of the world according to the foreknowledge of God, on account of their exceeding faith and good works.

Teaches: We existed, participated, and were prepared before we came. Job’s trial is happening inside a plan he was present for.
Job 38:4–11

Question after question about creation that Job cannot answer—foundations, measures, the sea’s boundaries.

Helaman 12:7–15

Mormon marvels that man is less than the dust of the earth, which moves at God’s command—the earth, the hills, the sea, and the sun all obey His voice.

Teaches: Both passages use creation obedience to reset human self-importance—and both do it to produce awe rather than shame.
Job 38:26

God sends rain on wilderness where no man is—care extended to ground nobody uses.

2 Nephi 26:24–25

He doeth nothing save it be for the benefit of the world; He invites all, denies none, black and white, bond and free, male and female.

Teaches: God’s care is not allocated by usefulness or by who is watching. That is exactly what Job needed to see.
Job 40:3–5

“I will lay mine hand upon my mouth”—Job stops arguing without yet claiming to understand.

1 Nephi 11:17

Nephi answers that he knows God loves His children, but he does not know the meaning of all things.

Teaches: Faith and unanswered questions can occupy the same person. Nephi says out loud the posture Job arrives at.
Job 42:5

“I have heard of thee by the hearing of the ear: but now mine eye seeth thee.”

Ether 3:19–20

The brother of Jared could not be kept from within the veil because of his knowledge—he had faith no longer, for he knew, and he saw the Lord.

Teaches: Both men move from secondhand belief to firsthand knowledge, and in both cases the move happens after the hardest part, not instead of it.
Job 42:8–10

Job’s captivity is turned when he prays for the three friends who accused him for thirty chapters.

1 Nephi 7:19–21

Nephi, having just been bound and left for dead by his brothers, frankly forgives them and prays that they will be forgiven.

Teaches: Praying for people who have hurt you is not the last step of healing. In both accounts it is the first.
Job 42:10–12

The Lord gives Job twice what he had, and blesses his latter end more than his beginning.

2 Nephi 2:1–2

Lehi tells Jacob that God will consecrate his afflictions for his gain—the suffering itself becomes the raw material of the blessing.

Teaches: The ending is not compensation tacked onto a bad experience. Affliction is consecrated, meaning it is put to use rather than merely paid off.
Job 42:15

Job’s daughters are named while his sons are not, and they receive an inheritance among their brothers.

2 Nephi 26:33

All are alike unto God—He inviteth them all to come unto Him and partake of His goodness, male and female alike.

Teaches: The epilogue quietly enacts a principle it never states. Worth pausing on with a class that includes young women.

Tie to Come, Follow Me

This week’s outline: “Yet Will I Trust in Him”—Job 1–3; 12–14; 19; 21–24; 38–40; 42, August 10–16, 2026. This handout covers the manual’s fourth principle and closes the week.

The manual sets it up honestly: frustrated with his friends’ accusations (Job 16:1–5; 19:1–3), Job repeatedly asked God why he had to suffer so much (19:6–7; 23:1–9; 31). The Lord did not give a clear reason in the book of Job. But He did teach an important message in chapters 38–40—and the manual asks students to summarize what that message is.

What the manual asks students to do

  • Summarize the Lord’s message. How would you sum up what God taught in chapters 38–40? Start with the questions in Job 38:1–7 and 18–24.
  • Go look at the creations. The Lord spoke to Job about His creations and mighty works (Job 38–39). Look at some of them, or pictures of them. How do these things change how you think about God? About your trials?
  • Track the change in Job. How did the Lord’s questions change Job’s perspective? (Job 42:1–6; compare Moses 1:8–10, where Moses sees the world and every inhabitant and afterward says he never supposed man was nothing.)
  • Use the manual’s “Be creative” suggestion. Learning does not have to be only reading, thinking, and talking. Take a walk and look at the Lord’s creations, large and small—or look at a single object from different perspectives: above, below, up close, far away.
The two conference talks the outline names

Tamara W. Runia, “Seeing God’s Family through the Overview Lens” (Liahona, Nov. 2023, 62–69), and President Russell M. Nelson, “Think Celestial!” (Liahona, Nov. 2023, 117–20). The manual’s instruction: in either message, look for something you will want to remember the next time your trials seem overwhelming—and decide how you will remind yourself of it. That last clause is an assignment, not a flourish.

Teaching ideas for youth Sunday School (ages 12–13)

  • Sixty-six questions, rapid fire. Read eight of the Lord’s questions from Job 38 out loud fast and have the class try to answer them. They cannot. Then ask what God was doing—He never explained Job’s suffering. Land on the difference between getting an answer and getting a perspective.
  • The one-object exercise. Pass around a single ordinary object—a rock, a key, a leaf. Look at it from above, underneath, an inch away, across the room. Same object, four true descriptions. That is chapters 38–42 in ninety seconds, and it is the manual’s own suggestion.
  • Show the creations. Pull up photos of the specific things God names: the Pleiades, an ostrich running, an eagle’s cliff nest, a wild donkey, snow crystals. Ask the manual’s question—how do these change how you think about your own hard week?
  • Ask what the animals have in common. After reading a few, ask which of them work for humans. None do. Then read 38:26—God waters land where nobody lives. Ask what that means about whether God’s care depends on you being useful or being watched.
  • Teach 38:7 as a doorway. Most of the class will not have noticed that a premortal scene sits in the middle of the Old Testament. Read it, explain the Council in Heaven, and ask what it means that they were there and were glad about a plan that included hard things.
  • The verdict surprise. Before reading 42:7, ask the class to predict who God is angry with. Most will guess Job. Read the verse. The man who complained for thirty chapters is the one who spoke rightly. This single moment does more for a doubting student than an hour of reassurance.
  • The prayer that turns it. Point out the order in 42:10—restoration begins when Job prays for the friends who wronged him, not before. Ask who they would have to pray for if that were the condition.
  • Close on 42:5. Ask what the difference is between hearing about someone and seeing them, and what would have to happen this year for “now mine eye seeth thee” to be a little more true of them.
Closing the week

If you are teaching all four parts in sequence, this is where the arc lands. Job asked why and never received a reason—he received a Person, a perspective, and a restoration. Elder Dale G. Renlund has observed, in substance, that the book never answers the why; what Job learns is that trusting God rather than leaning on his own understanding is how to approach difficulty, and that not knowing everything is a deliberate feature of mortal experience. Send them out with that rather than with a tidy explanation, because a tidy explanation is precisely what the book withholds.

Tie to Jesus Christ

Job never learned why. He met Someone instead—and the voice in the whirlwind has a name.

The voice in the whirlwind is His voice

The God who answers Job speaks as the Creator—the One who laid the foundations of the earth while the morning stars sang. Scripture identifies that Creator as Jehovah, Jesus Christ, acting under the Father’s direction. Which means the Being challenging Job to consider how little he understands about suffering is the very Being who would come down, take a body, and endure more of it than Job did.Job 38:4–7 · John 1:1–3 · Mosiah 3:8 · 3 Nephi 9:15 · Helaman 14:12

He speaks from the storm—and He stops storms

The Lord’s appearance to Job comes out of a whirlwind. Centuries later, in a boat on the Sea of Galilee, the same voice said three words to a storm and it went flat calm, and the men with Him asked what manner of man this was. Job met the God who commands the wind. The disciples met Him with a face. The manual’s own artwork for this section is a painting of Christ in a boat beneath a starry sky—which is exactly the connection.Job 38:1 · Mark 4:37–41 · Matthew 8:26–27

The plan the morning stars sang about

The joy in Job 38:7 was not joy about an easy life. The plan presented in that council required a Redeemer, and it required suffering that only a Redeemer could answer for. When Job is reminded that he was there, he is being reminded that he consented to a mortality with real risk in it—because a Savior was part of the arrangement.Job 38:7 · Abraham 3:22–27 · Moses 4:1–2 · Alma 34:8–10

“Now mine eye seeth thee”

Job’s turning point is a shift from hearing about God to seeing Him. That is what the Savior offers, and He offered it literally to a multitude at Bountiful—come forth, one by one, and see and feel for yourselves. Everything the gospel does, from covenants to the sacrament to the temple, is designed to move a person from hearsay to firsthand.Job 42:5 · 3 Nephi 11:14–15 · Ether 3:19–20 · D&C 93:1

Restoration begins with intercession

The book’s quiet hinge is Job 42:10: Job’s captivity turns when he prays for the friends who wronged him. On the cross, the Savior did the same for the men executing Him, and He stands as Advocate doing it still. Job received double—and Elder Dale G. Renlund has taught, in substance, that Christ delights to restore what we cannot restore and to compensate for unfairness we were powerless to prevent. That is not a bonus at the end of a bad story. It is inheritance.Job 42:10 · Luke 23:34 · D&C 45:3–5 · Isaiah 61:7 · 2 Nephi 2:2
