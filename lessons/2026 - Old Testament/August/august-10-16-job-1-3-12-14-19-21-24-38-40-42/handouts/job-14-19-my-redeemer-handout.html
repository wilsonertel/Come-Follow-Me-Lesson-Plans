1

Historical Summary of the Period

A note on the grouping. The manual assigns Job 12–14 as a block and builds its second principle on Job 19. Chapter 14 sits naturally here rather than with the rebuttal, because 14:14 asks the exact question 19:25–27 answers—and because the manual’s own children’s section pairs Job 14:14 with the Resurrection. Read together, these two chapters are the doctrinal summit of the book.

The institution behind the word “redeemer.” When Job says redeemer, he is not reaching for a poetic title. He is using a legal term. The Hebrew go’el was a specific family office: the nearest able relative, who carried both the right and the duty to buy back what a family member had lost. A go’el repurchased ancestral land that had been sold off, bought a relative out of debt-slavery, pursued justice when a family member was killed, and in some cases married a childless widow to preserve the family name. It is the role Boaz fills for Naomi and Ruth. The word carries the assumption of kinship—you could only be redeemed by someone related to you.

Why this makes Job 19:25 remarkable

Look at Job’s situation when he says it. His brethren are far off, his acquaintances estranged, his kinsfolk have failed, his servants ignore him, his wife recoils from him, and young children despise him (19:13–19). He has just catalogued the complete collapse of every family tie he has. There is no living go’el left to redeem him—and in that exact moment he declares that he has one anyway, that this Redeemer lives, and that He will stand upon the earth in the latter day. Job is not describing a relative. He is describing the Lord.

Writing technology in Job 19:23–24. Job wishes his words were written in a book, and then goes further—graven with an iron pen and lead in the rock forever. Commentators have long read this against real ancient practice: letters cut deep into stone with an iron stylus and the incisions then filled with molten lead, so wind and weather could not erase them. Rock-cut inscriptions on cliff faces were common across Sinai and Arabia; classical writers described inscribed stone pillars in Arabia bearing characters no one could still read. Job is asking for the most permanent medium his world knew of. Three thousand years later, his words are in more languages than any inscription ever carved.

What death looked like to Job’s neighbors. This is the context that makes chapter 14 ache. In the surrounding cultures the afterlife was a shadowy, diminished existence—the Mesopotamian netherworld was a house of dust from which no one returned, and the great epics treat the search for immortality as a doomed errand. Israelite writers before the prophets speak of Sheol in similarly muted terms. That is why Job’s question in 14:14 is genuinely open, and why his answer in 19:26 is so startling: not that his spirit survives somewhere dim, but that in his flesh he will see God.

The tree that Job envies. Chapter 14 turns on an image from agriculture that would have been unmistakable in Uz. Cut down an olive or a terebinth and the stump is not finished—at the scent of water it sends up new shoots and lives again. Job’s complaint is that a tree gets that and a man apparently does not (14:7–12). He is not denying resurrection. He is asking, with real longing, whether the mercy shown to a stump extends to a person. Keep that image live in class; the Restoration answers it directly.

Where this sits in the argument. Chapter 19 comes in the second cycle of speeches, after Bildad has just described the fate of the wicked in terms clearly aimed at Job. Job’s response is not a calm sermon. It is a man swinging between accusation (“God hath overthrown me,” 19:6), a plea for pity from friends who have offered none (19:21), and—out of nowhere and grounded in nothing visible—the most confident testimony of a living Redeemer in the Old Testament.

2

Passage Summary

Two chapters, one question and its answer. Chapter 14 asks; chapter 19 answers—from a worse position than the one that raised the question.

1
Job 14:1–6

Few days, and full of trouble

Job widens from his own case to the human condition: man is of few days, comes forth like a flower and is cut down, flees like a shadow. He asks God simply to look away and let a hired man finish his day in peace. It is exhausted, not rebellious.

2
Job 14:7–12

There is hope of a tree

The image that runs the chapter: cut a tree down and the stump will sprout again at the scent of water. But a man lies down and does not rise—“till the heavens be no more.” Note that last phrase carefully. Even inside the complaint, Job leaves a door open.

3
Job 14:13–15

“Thou shalt call, and I will answer”

Job asks to be hidden in the grave until God’s wrath is past, then remembered. He imagines God calling him and himself answering—and God longing for the work of His own hands. This is resurrection language, spoken as a wish.

4
Job 14:14

The question the Restoration answers

“If a man die, shall he live again?” Job says he will wait all the days of his appointed time until his change comes. He does not have the answer. He has the question and he has patience, which is more than his friends have offered him.

5
Job 14:16–22

The mountain that erodes

Job closes the chapter darkly: water wears away stones, floods wash away the soil, and God likewise wears down a man’s hope. He ends where he is—in pain, and mourning. The chapter does not resolve. Chapter 19 will.

6
Job 19:1–12

“How long will ye vex my soul?”

Ten times, Job says, his friends have reproached him. He protests that even if he has erred, the error is his own business, and charges that God has fenced his path, stripped his glory, and set him up as an enemy. This is the raw material out of which the testimony comes.

7
Job 19:13–20

The complete collapse of kinship

Read this list slowly in class: brethren far off, acquaintances estranged, kinsfolk failed, familiar friends forgetting him, servants not answering when called, his breath strange to his wife, young children despising him. He escapes with the skin of his teeth—a phrase Job coined that we still use.

8
Job 19:21–22

“Have pity upon me, O ye my friends”

The plea at the center of the chapter. He asks why they persecute him as God does and are not satisfied with his flesh. It is the last thing he asks of any human being in this speech.

9
Job 19:23–24

“Oh that my words were now written!”

Having given up on being heard by the people in front of him, Job wants a record. In a book. Better—cut into rock with an iron pen and sealed with lead, permanent. He wants the case on file for whoever reads it later. He got what he asked for.

10
Job 19:25–27

“I know that my redeemer liveth”

Then the summit: he knows his Redeemer lives and will stand at the latter day upon the earth; that though his skin be destroyed, in his flesh he shall see God; that he will see Him for himself, with his own eyes—and he adds, “though my reins be consumed within me,” which is Hebrew for this is killing me to say. Testimony borne out of the depths, not the heights.

11
Job 19:28–29

A warning to the friends

He closes by telling them to be afraid of the sword themselves, because there is a judgment. Chapter 42 will show he was right about that too—though when it comes, Job will be the one praying for them.

Translation note · “worms destroy this body”

In Job 19:26, the words worms and body are italicized in the King James text, meaning translators supplied them; the Hebrew is compressed and difficult. Most readings run something like “after my skin has been thus destroyed, yet from my flesh shall I see God.” The uncertainty is about the phrasing, not the substance—every serious reading has Job expecting to see God after his body has been ruined. Worth mentioning if a student notices the italics, and a good moment to teach what italics mean in the KJV.

“For I know that my redeemer liveth.”Job 19:25

Comparison to the Book of Mormon

Job reached for a doctrine he could not yet see clearly. The Book of Mormon supplies the outline of exactly what he was reaching for.

Job 14:14

“If a man die, shall he live again?”—asked as an open question, with no answer supplied.

Alma 11:42–44

Amulek describes the Resurrection in specific detail: every limb and joint restored to its perfect frame, and all brought to stand before God to be judged.

Teaches: Job’s deepest question receives a direct, physical, specific answer. The hope he reached for is doctrine we are handed.
Job 14:7–9

Hope for a tree: cut it down and the stump sprouts again at the scent of water. Job wonders whether people get the same mercy.

Jacob 5:3–7, 23–25

Zenos’s allegory: the master of the vineyard refuses to give up on a decaying tree, grafts, digs, and nourishes, and life returns to what looked finished.

Teaches: God’s pattern with living things is restoration, not disposal. The mercy shown to the stump is the mercy shown to us.
Job 19:25

“I know that my redeemer liveth”—the go’el, the kinsman with the right to buy back what was lost.

2 Nephi 2:6–9

Redemption comes in and through the Holy Messiah, who offers Himself a sacrifice and makes intercession for all.

Teaches: Redemption is a family word. Christ qualifies as our kinsman-redeemer because He took our flesh—He is related to us.
Job 19:26

“Yet in my flesh shall I see God”—not a shadowy survival, but a real body and a real seeing.

3 Nephi 11:14–15

The Savior invites twenty-five hundred people to come forward one by one and thrust their hands into His side and feel the prints in His hands and feet.

Teaches: What Job hoped for in the abstract, a whole multitude did with their hands. Resurrection is physical, and it is verifiable.
Job 19:23–24

Job wants his testimony engraved in rock with an iron pen so it survives him.

Jacob 4:1–3

Jacob describes the labor of engraving on plates—difficult work—so that future generations will receive their words with thankful hearts.

Teaches: Job asked for engraved testimony. The Book of Mormon is engraved testimony. Both got read by people the writers never met.
Job 19:13–20

Every relationship fails at once—family, friends, servants, wife. Job is socially as well as physically destroyed.

Mosiah 18:8–10

The baptismal covenant: bear one another’s burdens, mourn with those that mourn, stand as witnesses at all times and in all places.

Teaches: What happened to Job is exactly what a covenant community exists to prevent. This is why ministering assignments are not administrative busywork.
Job 19:21

“Have pity upon me, O ye my friends”—a request his friends never granted.

Alma 7:11–13

Christ takes upon Himself pains, afflictions, sicknesses, and infirmities so that He will know, according to the flesh, how to succor His people.

Teaches: The pity Job begged from friends who could not give it, the Savior possesses by experience. He is not sympathizing from a distance.
Job 14:1–2

Man is of few days and full of trouble; he comes forth like a flower and is cut down.

Alma 34:32–34

This life is the time to prepare to meet God; do not procrastinate the day of your repentance until the end.

Teaches: Both texts start from the shortness of life. Job draws grief from it; Amulek draws urgency. Held together, they produce seriousness without despair.

Tie to Come, Follow Me

This week’s outline: “Yet Will I Trust in Him”—Job 1–3; 12–14; 19; 21–24; 38–40; 42, August 10–16, 2026. This handout covers the manual’s second principle, and it is the one the manual treats most devotionally: Jesus Christ is my Redeemer.

What the manual asks students to do with Job 19

  • Ponder the contrast inside the chapter. The manual asks students to read the trials Job described in 19:1–22 alongside the truths he proclaimed in verses 23–27. The point is the contrast—the testimony comes from the low place, not after it.
  • Make it personal. Two direct questions from the manual: How do you know that your Redeemer lives? What difference does that knowledge make in your life?
  • Use the hymn. Consider singing or listening to “I Know That My Redeemer Lives” (Hymns, no. 136), then ask which words express your own feelings about Him. The manual recommends this hymn in both the youth and the children’s sections.
  • Follow the cross-references. The outline points to Doctrine and Covenants 121:1–12 and 122—the Lord’s answer to Joseph Smith in Liberty Jail, which names Job directly.
  • The manual’s framing line for the whole section: sometimes we learn the most important truths during our most difficult times.
Scripture Helps question for this section

The outline links the question “What might Job have meant when he said, ‘I know that my redeemer liveth’?” The answer given: in Old Testament times a redeemer was someone—usually a relative—responsible for restoring a person’s possessions, standing in society, or personal freedom. By testifying of his Redeemer, Job was expressing faith that he would ultimately be vindicated and that justice would be restored. Elder Dale G. Renlund has taught, in substance, that the Savior delights to restore what we cannot restore, to heal wounds we cannot heal, to repair what is irreparably broken, and to compensate for any unfairness we have endured (“Trust God and Let Him Prevail,” Liahona, Aug. 2022).

Teaching ideas for youth Sunday School (ages 12–13)

  • Teach the word redeemer before you read the verse. Explain the kinsman-redeemer: the relative who could buy back your land, your freedom, your name. Ask who in Job’s life could still do that for him—then read 19:13–19 and let them find that the answer is nobody. Then read verse 25. The verse lands three times harder in that order.
  • Read the abandonment list as a list. Have eight students each read one relationship from 19:13–19 aloud in turn—brethren, acquaintances, kinsfolk, friends, servants, wife, children. Hearing it stack up does what a summary cannot.
  • Carve it in rock. Job wanted his testimony engraved forever (19:23–24). Give each student a card and a minute to write one true line about the Savior. Then tell them Job’s wish came true—his sentence is in scripture, in a hymn, and in Handel’s Messiah—and ask what they would want carved.
  • The stump object lesson. Bring a photo of a cut tree stump with new shoots coming out of it. Read Job 14:7–10 and ask what Job envied about that tree. Then read Alma 11:42–44 as the answer to his question. Concrete, fast, and it sticks.
  • Answer Job directly. Write Job 14:14 on the board as a question addressed to the class, and have them answer it in one sentence each, using what they know. This is the manual’s own suggestion for children and it works just as well at thirteen.
  • Explain KJV italics. Show them verse 26—“worms” and “body” are in italics because translators added them. Teaching this once pays off for the rest of their scripture study, and it models honest handling of a hard text.
  • Close by singing verse one of hymn 136 and asking which phrase they would have wanted Job to hear on the ash heap.

5

Tie to Jesus Christ

Every other section of this week points toward the Savior. This one names Him outright, from the mouth of a man who had no reason left to hope.

The kinsman-redeemer

The go’el had to be a relative—that was the whole basis of the right. It is why the Savior’s mortal birth is not a detail but a qualification. He took flesh and blood, entered a family, was born under the law, and became our nearest kinsman, so that He would hold the legal right to buy back what we lost. Job’s title for Him is not sentimental. It is a claim about jurisdiction.Job 19:25 · Ruth 2:20; 4:9–10 · Hebrews 2:11–17 · Mosiah 3:7–9

“And that he shall stand at the latter day upon the earth”

Job does not only say his Redeemer lives; he says He will stand upon the earth at the latter day. That is a prophecy of a physically embodied, returning Christ, spoken centuries before Isaiah and millennia before the Second Coming. Job’s hope is not that things will improve. It is that a Person will arrive.Job 19:25 · Zechariah 14:4 · Acts 1:11 · D&C 45:44–53

The answer to Job 14:14

Job asked whether a man who dies will live again, imagined God calling and himself answering, and said he would wait for his change to come. The empty tomb is the answer, and it is not a general reassurance—it is precisely the thing he described. Because Christ rose in a body, Job will rise in one, and will see God in his flesh exactly as he said.Job 14:14–15 · Job 19:26 · John 11:25–26 · Alma 11:42–44 · 1 Corinthians 15:20

He restores what we cannot

A go’el’s work was restoration: land back, freedom back, name back. Job lost ten children, and the epilogue gives him ten more—which is only a real answer if the first ten are not permanently gone. Sealing and resurrection are what make Job 42 a restoration rather than a replacement. Elder Dale G. Renlund has taught, in substance, that the Savior loves to restore what we cannot restore and to make up for unfairness we had no power to prevent.Job 42:10–13 · Isaiah 61:7 · D&C 138:15–19 · Alma 40:23

Testimony from the bottom

The most important thing about Job 19:25 may be where it was spoken: not from a pulpit, not after the restoration in chapter 42, but from an ash heap, with nothing resolved and no evidence in hand. Job did not know why he suffered and never found out. He knew a Redeemer lived. For a thirteen-year-old going through something hard, that is the model—you do not need the explanation to have the testimony.Job 19:23–27 · Ether 12:6 · D&C 121:7–8 · 1 Nephi 11:17
